**Short answer:** in claims, machine learning works best as a reliable first opinion: a cost estimate, a set of extracted fields, a fraud-risk score. It speeds up adjusters without taking the decision away from them. The hard parts are data, validation and trust, not the algorithm.

## Where ML creates value in claims

- **Cost and severity prediction.** Gradient-boosting models trained on historical claims can estimate the likely cost of a new claim from damage, vehicle, policy and context features.
- **Document understanding.** LLMs and document AI turn forms, reports and correspondence into structured fields, so nobody has to re-type them.
- **Fraud and anomaly detection.** Models rank claims by risk, so investigators look at the most suspicious cases first.
- **Triage and routing.** Simple, fast-track claims get separated from complex ones.

## What makes it work in practice

### Start from the decision

Define exactly which decision the model supports and what an error costs. Overestimating a reserve and underestimating it have different consequences; the model and its thresholds should reflect that.

### Validate on the future

Claims data shifts over time: prices, repair practices and fraud patterns change. Validate models on later periods than the ones used for training, and monitor drift once deployed.

### Explain every prediction

Adjusters adopt a model when they can see why it suggests a figure. Per-claim explanations of the main cost drivers turn a black box into a second opinion they can challenge.

### Close the loop

Every correction an adjuster makes is new training data. Capture it, and the model improves with use.

## Lessons from coordinating a multi-company programme

On large programmes, data ownership is spread across companies and departments. A shared data dictionary, clear owners for each source and regular demos to business stakeholders matter as much as model accuracy.

## Where to start

If you are an insurer or a claims-management company, the fastest path is usually a focused pilot on one claim type with a clear KPI, such as estimate time or estimate consistency. See how I approach it in the [insurance claims case study](/work/insurance-claims-machine-learning/).
